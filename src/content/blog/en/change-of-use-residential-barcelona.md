Turning offices, commercial premises or disused buildings into homes is one of the most sensible moves in Barcelona: there is demand, there are under-used buildings, and the result often multiplies the asset's value. But **not every building can change use**, and those that can do not always allow as many homes as it seems.

These are the points we check, in this order.

## 1. Planning must allow residential use

First, the plot's zoning in the Metropolitan General Plan and any plans that affect it. You need to confirm that residential use is permitted **on that plot and on that floor**. Ground floors and basements have stricter conditions, and semi-basements or below-ground spaces are, in practice, ruled out as homes.

## 2. Density: how many homes really fit

Planning caps the **maximum number of homes** per plot. So the first calculation is not how many homes fit on plan, but how many are still available in the building.

In a recent study on Carrer Muntaner for an investment fund, the plot allowed 39 homes and already had 31. Converting five offices into homes was viable because there was margin; without it, it would not have mattered how well they fitted on plan.

## 3. Habitability standards

Each resulting home must meet the Catalan government's habitability standards (Decree 141/2012) to obtain a **habitability certificate**:

- Minimum usable floor area and clear ceiling height.
- Minimum layout: living room, kitchen, bathroom and bedroom with regulated dimensions.
- **Natural ventilation and daylight** in the main rooms, from the façade or from a light well of sufficient size.

In deep-plan offices the problem is rarely floor area: it is getting light and air into every room. That is where the real number of homes is decided.

## 4. Accessibility, fire safety and energy efficiency

A change of use requires adapting the building to the regulations for the new use: the Spanish Building Code (fire safety, health, energy saving, noise) and accessibility. This may mean a new lift, better insulation or completely new services.

## 5. Owners' association

If the building is under horizontal property, check the **community rules**: they may restrict how private units are used. If the change also involves subdividing or altering shares, the deed of division will need amending with the agreements required by the Catalan Civil Code. This is a legal point best reviewed with a lawyer alongside the technical work.

## 6. Affordable-housing reserve

In Barcelona, residential schemes above a certain size, both new build and major rehabilitation, may be required to set aside part of the floor area for affordable housing. Check whether it applies and on what terms it is in force when you plan the deal, because it changes the return completely.

## 7. Permit and timelines

A change of use with works is processed as a **building permit** with the City Council, with a design signed by an architect. If the building is listed, a heritage report is added.

Indicative timelines we see in practice in Barcelona:

- **Feasibility and pre-consultations:** 3 to 6 weeks.
- **Basic and detailed design:** 2 to 4 months depending on size.
- **Permit:** several months; it depends heavily on the district, on heritage and on the quality of the application.
- **Construction:** 6 to 14 months for a whole building.

On completion, the **first occupancy** sign-off and habitability certificates are obtained, and the new use is recorded at the Land Registry.

## In short

A well-planned conversion to residential starts with three numbers: **homes planning allows, homes that meet habitability, and the cost of getting there**. If they line up, the deal makes sense.
